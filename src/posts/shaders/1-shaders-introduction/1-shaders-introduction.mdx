# Shaders Introduction

It's very hard for me to put into words how much I love shaders, so instead I'm going to tell you how much they made me suffer!

Alright, '_suffer_' might be too harsh of a word, but I can definitely say that I didn't always have a great time in my shader learning process.


#### Shaders are intimidating

<Separator />

Let's say that you just heard about this cool new "shaders" thing. 
You search the term on the internet and find yourself in awe with just how great Minecraft can look.
You install a shader and enter the 2 week Minecraft phase.

Time passes and you grow to be a wiser person. 
The next time you search for shaders, you add "programming" to the query because now you actually want to learn how they work.
You find this cool [shadertoy](https://www.shadertoy.com/) website and you're struck with a different kind of awe.
Literal pieces of art, expanding, moving, self-replicating, _infinite_.

I'll just let the images speak for themselves:

But the real art is the code behind those visuals. They say art is meant to be admired, not understood, which is lucky because, _Oh My God_, look at this thing:

```glsl title="The mountain shape in the Cyber Fuji shader"
    vec2 k1 = vec2(r2,he);
    vec2 k2 = vec2(r2-r1,2.0*he);
    p.x = abs(p.x);
    vec2 ca = vec2(p.x-min(p.x,(p.y<0.0)?r1:r2), abs(p.y)-he);
    vec2 cb = p - k1 + k2*clamp( dot(k1-p,k2)/dot2(k2), 0.0, 1.0 );
    float s = (cb.x<0.0 && ca.y<0.0) ? -1.0 : 1.0;
    return s*sqrt( min(dot2(ca),dot2(cb)) );
```

Less lucky, though, is that your GPU doesn't have an appreciation for aesthetics and would rather delect itself with the actual code.

>If you're tempted to close the tab now, I don't blame you, I also had to take a break after looking at that code.
>But before you do, I'll tell you a secret: **It's not actually as impossible as it looks**.
>
>I spent a long time feeling stuck until I realized that shaders are actually much closer to a tool you probably already use every day.
>We'll get to that in a second, but first, let's talk about the elephant in the room.


#### Shaders use lots of math.. which is also intimidating

<Separator />

Now, I am the kind of person that likes math, but even for someone like me, it was pretty hard to get into it,
especially since you forget a lot when not in contact with a subject. And again, _I like math_, but it still felt that way. 
It's hard to imagine what someone who dislikes math feels when getting in this position.

It's alright, though, there are probably lots of great resources to learn from. 
For example, the golden standard most people hear about is [The Book of Shaders](https://thebookofshaders.com/) by Patricio Gonzalez Vivo.
It is an extraordinarily useful slice of knowledge and it's in GLSL, so it's easily translatable to Godot Shaders.

However, I don't think it's a good resource to start with for beginners. At least, it wasn't for me. 
For some reason, once I reached the 5th page of the book, it was pretty hard to continue.

I imagine how this must feel, especially if you don't have a spinning chair. But let me tell you:

<Align align="center"><h2>YOU DO NOT NEED MATH IN ORDER TO WRITE GOOD SHADERS!\*</h2></Align>

Remember how I said shaders are like a tool you already use? If you've ever opened Photoshop, GIMP, or any image editor, you already understand the core principles.

Let me give you a concrete example. Take a look at the mix function's description:

```glsl
Mix performs a linear interpolation between x and y 
using a to weight between them. 
The return value is computed as x*(1−a)+y*a.
```

You probably could digest this definition, try to understand it, memorize it and find its use cases. 
However, there's a much simpler way to think about this function: a gradient tool!

<Media label={`Gimp Gradient`}>
    <img src={gimpGradient} alt="Gimp Gradient"/>
</Media>

Yup, that tool you have in all image editing programs. That's it! You have two colors and a weight.
I bet that this representation of the _mix_ function will stay with you for longer than the definition I provided earlier.

This analogy works so well because it's also true the other way around. Your image editing tools have math behind.

If you apply a multiply blending mode to a layer, what does that do? It literally multiplies pixel colors with what's below.

<Media label={`Multiply`} width="680px">
    <img src={multiplyEffect} alt="Color Multiply Effect"/>
</Media>

If you use levels, you're pretty much using an <Hint message={"This is pretty much a re-scaling. When you move the pointers in a levels tab you're telling the program something like: 'Hey, the left pointer is the new black, the right pointer is perfect white. Figure out everything in between."}>inverse lerp</Hint> on that image.

<Media label={`Gimp Levels`}>
    <img src={gimpLevels} alt="Gimp Levels"/>
</Media>

And pretty much every little button and slider you see is, most probably, just some math in disguise.

So then, if most tools in a drawing app are just math behind the scenes, why not think of math functions inside shaders as just being some tools?
Following this perspective makes everything easier to understand:
- The _step()_ function? That's a Layer Mask.
- The _mix()_ function? That's the gradient tool we were talking about.
- UV coordinates? That's just your canvas.
- Mathematical functions? Those are just the brushes you draw with.
- The guy giving too many examples? That's me!

You get the point!
